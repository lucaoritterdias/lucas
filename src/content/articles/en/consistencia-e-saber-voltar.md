## The part nobody publishes

Consistency is sh*t.

Not the polished version, arranged into calendars and shown after it worked. Real consistency: opening the project after the idea has lost its novelty, revising for the fourth time what looked finished, and doing what you promised on a day when nobody will notice.

Starting brings its own energy. There is curiosity, urgency, and the comforting image of the final result. Continuing is different. After a few weeks, the work stops offering an immediate reward. Progress becomes hard to see, and every repetition looks too small to matter.

That is when almost every important project begins to depend on uninteresting tasks:

- talking to one more customer after you think you understand the problem;
- publishing when the text feels merely good, not brilliant;
- repairing the foundation before adding the next feature;
- tracking a metric even when it has not changed;
- resuming something without turning the pause into a judgment about yourself.

Extraordinary outcomes usually stand on a long infrastructure of ordinary actions. We see the launch, the promotion, the growing company, or the working product. We do not see the dozens of Tuesdays when someone did the work without applause or certainty.

## Perfect streaks are fragile

We often measure consistency with a streak: I trained for 40 days, published every week, studied for six months without missing once. A streak is motivating while it remains intact. Once broken, it can become a trap.

If the goal was never to fail, one bad day turns the entire history into defeat. Someone who showed up on 39 out of 40 days starts seeing only the one they missed. After the first failure, the second costs less. Before long, an interruption becomes abandonment.

A more useful definition is this: **consistency means shortening the time between leaving the path and returning to it**.

That definition accepts life as it is. Customers have crises, children get sick, priorities change, energy runs out. The system does not need to prevent every interruption. It needs to prevent one bad Tuesday from deciding the next three months.

So, along with frequency, I track a less glamorous measure: **return time**.

| Situation | Fragile metric | Useful metric |
|---|---|---|
| Writing | consecutive days writing | time between dropping and reopening the draft |
| Product | deliveries with no delays | time to reorganize the plan after a delay |
| Exercise | perfect streak | how often one missed day became two |
| Customer relationships | never receiving a complaint | time between hearing the problem and answering clearly |
| Learning | hours per day | weeks with at least one focused session |

Frequency creates volume. Return time creates resilience. To continue for years, we need both.

## The minimum must fit the worst day

Plans are usually made by our most optimistic self. They slept well, have an empty calendar, and believe every coming week will look the same. That person commits to two daily hours of study, three weekly posts, and a routine that starts at five in the morning.

Ambition is not the problem. Designing a system that only works under ideal conditions is.

Every important commitment should have two versions:

1. **Full version:** what I do when I have time, attention, and energy.
2. **Minimum version:** the smallest action that keeps the work alive on a bad day.

For writing, the full version might mean finishing a section; the minimum, capturing one idea and the next sentence. For a product, the full version might be shipping a feature; the minimum, closing one pending decision and making the next step explicit. For learning, the full version might be an hour of practice; the minimum, solving one exercise with care.

The minimum does not exist to fake productivity. It lowers the cost of beginning again. A project abandoned for two weeks demands that you rebuild context, remember decisions, and overcome accumulated guilt. A project touched for fifteen minutes remains mentally available.

> [!NOTE]
> The minimum is a bridge, not a home. If it becomes the standard for many weeks, the problem is not discipline: the commitment has lost priority, is too large, or no longer makes sense. Renegotiate it honestly.

## Motivation should not be on the critical path

In software, we avoid placing an unstable dependency on a request's critical path. If every payment depends on a service that fails half the time, the entire system inherits that instability.

Yet we organize work as if motivation were dependable.

It is not. Motivation changes with sleep, recognition, difficulty, environment, and a dozen factors outside our control. Waiting to feel like working is designing a critical operation around a service with no SLA.

The replacement is not infinite willpower. It is removing decisions from the moment of execution:

- write down the next step before stopping;
- reserve a specific time instead of waiting for an opening;
- shrink deliverables until they fit into one session;
- make progress visible;
- create a commitment with someone affected by the missing delivery.

A useful rule is to end each session by answering: **what is the first physical action next time?** “Continue the article” is abstract. “Rewrite the two paragraphs in the motivation section” lets you begin without negotiating with yourself.

This looks minor, but much procrastination is not born from laziness. It is born from ambiguity. When work has no clear entrance, arranging the desk, answering messages, or researching one more tool can look like progress.

## Evidence beats identity

There is a distance between saying “I want to write” and seeing yourself as someone who writes. Affirmations do not cross it. Evidence does.

Every delivery is a small vote for the person and professional we are building. One vote changes little. Dozens begin to change how we decide. After publishing a few pieces, the next one stops being an experiment and becomes part of the work. After honoring difficult agreements, we trust our own word a little more.

The opposite is also true. Repeatedly overpromising and abandoning creates evidence that our plans cannot be trusted. The greater damage is not losing a week; it is learning not to believe the next commitment we make to ourselves.

That is why I prefer a modest cadence that survives over an ambitious one that requires heroics. Trust grows when promise and delivery remain close, not when the promise sounds impressive.

## A return protocol

When the cadence breaks — and it will — do not try to repay everything in one day. Emotional debt charges high interest: the larger the recovery plan, the harder it is to begin.

I use a simple protocol:

1. **Acknowledge it without drama.** The agreement was not kept. That is a fact, not an identity.
2. **Find the concrete friction.** Was it time, clarity, energy, skill, or priority? “Lack of discipline” is rarely a sufficient diagnosis.
3. **Cut the scope of the return.** Pick one action you can finish today that leaves the work better than you found it.
4. **Protect the next repetition.** Schedule when it will happen and prepare its starting point.
5. **Review after three cycles.** One return may be enthusiasm. Three repetitions show whether the system works again.

The first session is not meant to recover the delay. It is meant to recover the rhythm.

Imagine an article untouched for a month. The common impulse is to reserve an entire Saturday to finish, edit, and publish it. The task grows, no Saturday feels good enough, and the draft stays closed. A better return might be opening the file today, deleting a section that no longer works, and writing three lines about what comes next. Tomorrow, there is less context to rebuild and less shame to cross.

## Continuity also requires quitting

Not everything deserves to be maintained. Sometimes we preserve a routine because abandoning it feels inconsistent, even when the goal has changed or the cost no longer makes sense. That is not consistency; it is attachment to an old plan.

Being consistent requires choosing very few things. Every recurring “yes” occupies future space. Before making a commitment, it helps to answer:

- why must this exist long enough to compound?
- what frequency can I sustain without depending on a perfect week?
- what will I stop doing to make room?
- under which condition will I reduce, pause, or end it?

Exit criteria protect consistency because they stop dead commitments from draining the energy of living ones. Ending something consciously is different from disappearing. One is a decision. The other is accumulated postponement.

## Extraordinary is a delayed consequence

Consistency rarely looks important on the day it happens. One conversation, one workout, one improvement, and one written page are almost nothing. Their value appears when those units combine: the conversation improves the product, the product builds trust, and trust opens an opportunity that looks like luck to anyone who only saw the ending.

We cannot control when accumulated results become visible. We control whether there will be enough work for them to emerge from.

It is sh*t because we almost always need to act before confirmation. Repeat before knowing it will work. Continue after novelty is gone. Return without the comfortable story that this time we will be flawless.

But perhaps that is the advantage. Almost everyone can start while excitement is present. Few people build a way to continue after it leaves — and an even better way to return when life interrupts.

We do not need to win every day. We need to stop one lost day from choosing the next one.
