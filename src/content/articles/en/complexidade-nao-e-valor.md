## Technology should disappear

For the person using a product, the best technology is usually the kind that does not call attention to itself. The screen responds, information arrives, and work moves forward. Considerable effort may exist behind that simplicity, but it should not be transferred to the user as one more step, one more confirmation screen, one more decision that was never theirs to make.

When a system constantly needs explaining to be used, the problem is usually not a lack of training. It is too much technical decision-making leaking into the interface. Engineering that takes pride in itself on-screen has often charged that pride against the clarity of someone who just wanted to finish the task.

## Every choice creates maintenance

Every tool, integration, or layer added to a project creates a future responsibility. The question is not only whether something can be implemented, but whether it deserves to keep existing for years, being updated, monitored, and explained to whoever arrives later. This shift in perspective prevents technical enthusiasm from becoming operational cost.

It is easy to justify a new dependency by looking only at today's problem. It is harder, and more valuable, to ask who will update that piece two years from now, once the person who chose it has left the team. Experience, to a large extent, is remembering to ask that question before writing the first line.

## Simplicity requires experience

Simplifying is not doing less for lack of ability. It is understanding the problem well enough to remove what does not contribute. After many projects, I learned that the best decisions are rarely the loudest. They are the ones that leave the clearest path for whoever comes next, whether a user or another developer.

That usually requires more discussion, not less: understanding the reason behind each requirement before accepting it, questioning whether an exception is truly necessary or just convenient in the moment. Simplicity, done well, looks obvious once it is finished, but it is rarely the first path that appears.

## The invisible cost of exceptions

Every exception looks small when it is created. "Just this one client needs a different flow," "only in this case does the calculation change," "it's temporary until we solve that other problem." Individually, each one is reasonable. Together, they form a system where nobody can predict behavior with confidence, because the rules are no longer the same for everyone.

A sign of technical maturity is noticing this early: asking, before accepting an exception, whether it solves a real problem or simply postpones an uncomfortable conversation about what the product should actually do. Not every exception should be refused, but every exception should be deliberate, documented, and ideally have an expiration date.

## Simplifying is a business decision, not only a technical one

It is tempting to treat simplicity as an engineering preference, a taste for clean code. But every layer removed is also money and time preserved for the business: less support, less training, fewer people needed to operate the same system. Unnecessary complexity is not just an aesthetic problem; it is a recurring cost that shows up on the maintenance bill every month.

That is why decisions about simplicity should sit at the same table as product and business decisions. When that happens, "let's keep it simple" stops being a technical whim and becomes, more often than not, the financially responsible call.
